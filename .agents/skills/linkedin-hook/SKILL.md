# LinkedIn Hook

Write the first one or two lines of a post — the only part most people see before deciding to click "…see more." Everything rides on it. This skill produces hooks; it doesn't write the whole post unless asked.

## When to Use This Skill

- The user types `/linkedin-hook`, or asks for a hook, an opener, or "the first two lines".
- They have a post (or the idea for one) and want the top to stop the scroll.

## What Makes a Hook Work

The hook has one job: earn the next line. On LinkedIn the feed truncates after ~1–2 lines, so the opener must create enough tension that stopping feels worse than continuing.

**Get the input first.** Before writing, know:
- What's the post actually about — the point, the story, or the takeaway?
- Who's the audience, and what do they care about?
- Is there a real specific — a number, an outcome, a surprising fact, a personal moment? Specifics hook; generalities don't.

If the user hasn't given you the payoff, ask for it. A hook to nothing is clickbait, and it burns trust.

## Hook Patterns That Earn the Click

Offer a few, in different registers, so the user can pick what fits their voice:

- **The stakes/number** — "We lost $40k in one afternoon. Here's the mistake that caused it."
- **The contrarian take** — "Everyone says X. After [experience], I think the opposite."
- **The specific moment** — "A recruiter told me something on Friday I can't stop thinking about."
- **The before/after** — "Two years ago I couldn't [X]. Yesterday I [Y]."
- **The plain-stated result** — "I [did specific thing] and [specific outcome]. Three things I learned:"
- **The question that names a real tension** — not a lazy "Ever feel stuck?" but a sharp one the audience actually feels.

## Rules

1. **Line one is short.** It has to survive the truncation and read fast. Front-load the tension; cut every wind-up word ("So", "I wanted to share", "Recently").
2. **No bait-and-switch.** The hook must be paid off by the post. If it promises a number, the number appears.
3. **Specific beats clever.** A concrete detail out-pulls a witty abstraction almost every time.
4. **Match the user's voice.** If an `about-me` profile exists, honor its register — a hook can be attention-grabbing without being out of character. Skip the manufactured-guru tone unless that's genuinely how the user writes.
5. **No em-dash-and-emoji "AI-hook" template.** Avoid the "🚨 Unpopular opinion:" / "Let that sink in." clichés unless the user wants them.

## Output

Give **3–5 hook options**, each labeled with its pattern, so the user can compare. If they picked one, offer to write the transition into the body next.
